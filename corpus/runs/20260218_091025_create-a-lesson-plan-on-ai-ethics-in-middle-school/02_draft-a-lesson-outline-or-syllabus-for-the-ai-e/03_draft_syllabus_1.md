# AI Ethics - Syllabus

## Course Information

- **Duration:** 6 weeks
- **Modality:** Online

## Course Modules

### Module 1: Foundations of AI and Ethics
This module introduces the fundamental landscape of AI ethics, distinguishing the unique moral challenges posed by machine learning systems from those found in traditional software development.
- Differentiate between ethical challenges specific to artificial intelligence and traditional software development by identifying at least three distinct areas of risk.
- Participate in a collaborative discussion board comparing recent software failures to AI-specific failures.
- Complete a short reflection identifying potential risks in a specific AI tool of the student's choice.
- **Assessment:** Risk Identification Quiz

### Module 2: Bias in the Data Pipeline
Students examine the lifecycle of data to understand how and where bias is introduced, leading to discriminatory outcomes in algorithmic decision-making.
- Analyze a provided case study of an algorithmic failure to pinpoint the specific stage in the data pipeline where bias was introduced.
- Deconstruct a real-world case study (e.g., hiring algorithms or facial recognition) in a small group breakout session.
- Create a visual map of a data pipeline highlighting potential points of bias insertion.
- **Assessment:** Case Study Analysis Worksheet

### Module 3: Privacy, Surveillance, and Consent
This module focuses on the ethical implications of data collection methods, emphasizing the tension between utility and user privacy rights.
- Evaluate a contemporary AI application's data collection methods against fundamental privacy principles to determine if user consent is adequately protected.
- Conduct a "privacy audit" of a popular mobile application using a structured checklist.
- Engage in a debate regarding the ethics of surveillance capitalism and data scraping.
- **Assessment:** Privacy Evaluation Report

### Module 4: Explainability and Transparency
Students explore the "black box" problem in AI and the necessity of interpretability, particularly in high-stakes sectors like healthcare and criminal justice.
- Critique the use of opaque 'black box' models in high-stakes decision-making by proposing two specific strategies for increasing model explainability.
- Review technical documentation of various models to assess their current transparency levels.
- Draft a mock "model card" or explanation system intended for a non-technical user audience.
- **Assessment:** Explainability Strategy Proposal

### Module 5: Societal Impact and Workforce
The course shifts to macro-level analysis, forecasting how AI-driven automation will reshape industries, labor markets, and social structures.
- Predict the potential long-term societal impacts of AI-driven automation in a chosen industry, supporting the forecast with at least two evidence-based factors.
- Research specific industry trends and present findings in a synchronous or asynchronous micro-presentation.
- Simulate a stakeholder meeting to discuss mitigation strategies for workforce displacement.
- **Assessment:** Industry Impact Forecast Brief

### Module 6: Building Ethical Frameworks
In this capstone module, students synthesize prior learning to create actionable governance structures that prioritize accountability and fairness.
- Construct a set of ethical guidelines for a hypothetical AI project that explicitly addresses issues of accountability, transparency, and fairness.
- Review existing corporate and governmental AI ethical charters to identify best practices.
- Draft a comprehensive governance framework for a provided hypothetical scenario.
- **Assessment:** Final Ethical Guidelines Project
