# Grade 9 English — Complete Lesson Plan

## What You'll Learn

1. How to critically read, analyze, and interpret literary and informational texts across genres
2. How to construct evidence-based arguments, narratives, and research reports in formal academic writing
3. How to communicate effectively through collaborative discussion, oral presentations, and multimedia projects

---

## About This Course

Join this full-year Grade 9 English Language Arts course, designed to build your confidence as a reader, writer, speaker, and critical thinker. Through diverse texts — short stories, novels, poetry, speeches, and informational articles — you'll develop the analytical and communication skills that form the foundation for success in high school and beyond. Every module moves from guided instruction to independent practice, so you grow at every stage.

---

## Who Should Join?

- **Target Audience:** Grade 9 students entering high school English
- **Prerequisites:** Completion of Grade 8 ELA or equivalent; basic reading comprehension and paragraph-writing skills
- **Ideal for:** Students who want to strengthen literary analysis, argumentative writing, research skills, and public speaking

---

## Measurable Learning Objectives

Objectives are aligned to **Bloom's Taxonomy** and progress from foundational to higher-order thinking:

| # | Objective | Bloom's Level | Success Criteria |
|---|-----------|---------------|------------------|
| 1 | **Identify** key literary devices (metaphor, simile, personification, imagery) in a given text | Remember | At least 80% accuracy |
| 2 | **Summarize** the central theme and supporting details of a text | Understand | Well-organized paragraph of 5–7 sentences |
| 3 | **Analyze** how word choice and tone contribute to mood | Analyze | Cite at least 3 specific textual examples |
| 4 | **Compare and contrast** two characters' motivations and development | Analyze | Structured graphic organizer or essay |
| 5 | **Construct** a well-structured argumentative paragraph | Evaluate | Clear claim, textual evidence, logical reasoning |
| 6 | **Evaluate** the effectiveness of narrative perspective | Evaluate | Written response with 2+ pieces of evidence |
| 7 | **Create** an original short narrative (150–250 words) | Create | Incorporates at least 3 literary devices studied |

## Course Outline (6 Modules · 27+ Activities & Assessments)

### Module 1: The Power of Narrative (Weeks 1–6)

**Focus:** Short fiction, personal narrative, and narrative craft

**Description:** Students explore how authors construct narratives to convey meaning. Through close reading of short stories from diverse voices, students analyze point of view, conflict, characterization, and theme, then apply these techniques in their own narrative writing.

**Suggested Texts:**
- "The Most Dangerous Game" — Richard Connell
- "Thank You, Ma'am" — Langston Hughes
- *The House on Mango Street* (excerpts) — Sandra Cisneros

**Instructional Strategies:**
| Strategy | Description |
|---|---|
| Close Reading Protocols | Annotation-based reading with text-dependent questions |
| Writer's Workshop | Drafting, peer review, and revision cycles for personal narratives |
| Think-Pair-Share | Short collaborative discussions to unpack story elements |
| Mentor Text Analysis | Published narratives as models for student craft |

**Assessments:**
- Formative: Reading journals, annotation checks, exit tickets
- Summative: Personal narrative essay (3–5 pages) + literary analysis paragraph

---

### Module 2: Argument & Persuasion (Weeks 7–12)

**Focus:** Argumentative reading and writing, rhetoric, and evidence evaluation

**Description:** Students examine how writers and speakers build arguments. They identify claims, evidence, reasoning, and rhetorical appeals (ethos, pathos, logos) in mentor texts, speeches, and media, culminating in a formal argumentative essay.

**Suggested Texts:**
- "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (excerpts) — Martin Luther King Jr.
- Selected op-eds and editorials
- TED Talks and speech transcripts

**Instructional Strategies:**
| Strategy | Description |
|---|---|
| Socratic Seminar | Student-led discussions grounded in textual evidence |
| CER Framework | Claim-Evidence-Reasoning structured approach to argumentation |
| Gallery Walk | Analyzing posted arguments/ads for rhetorical strategies |
| Debate Protocols | Structured mini-debates for oral argumentation practice |

**Assessments:**
- Formative: Rhetorical analysis graphic organizers, CER practice paragraphs
- Summative: Argumentative essay (5 paragraphs, MLA format) + brief oral defense

---

### Module 3: The Novel Study — Identity & Society (Weeks 13–18)

**Focus:** Extended text analysis, theme development, and literary interpretation

**Description:** Deep study of a full-length novel exploring identity, belonging, and society. Students trace character development, analyze symbolism, and connect themes to broader human experiences through literature circles.

**Suggested Texts (choose 1–2):**
- *To Kill a Mockingbird* — Harper Lee
- *The Absolutely True Diary of a Part-Time Indian* — Sherman Alexie
- *A Raisin in the Sun* — Lorraine Hansberry

**Instructional Strategies:**
| Strategy | Description |
|---|---|
| Literature Circles | Rotating small-group roles (discussion director, passage picker, connector) |
| Dialectical Journals | Double-entry journals connecting evidence to analysis |
| Fishbowl Discussion | Inner/outer circle format for modeling literary discussion |
| Scaffolded Essay Writing | Step-by-step thesis development, outlining, drafting, revision |

**Assessments:**
- Formative: Weekly literature circle participation, dialectical journals, reading quizzes
- Summative: Literary analysis essay (thesis-driven, 4–6 paragraphs)

---

### Module 4: Poetry & Figurative Language (Weeks 19–24)

**Focus:** Poetry analysis, figurative language, sound devices, and creative expression

**Description:** Students explore poetry across eras and cultures, analyzing how poets use language, structure, and imagery. Students write original poems and deliver spoken word performances.

**Suggested Texts:**
- Poems by Langston Hughes, Maya Angelou, Robert Frost, Emily Dickinson
- Contemporary poets: Amanda Gorman, Naomi Shihab Nye, Billy Collins
- Spoken word / slam poetry videos

**Instructional Strategies:**
| Strategy | Description |
|---|---|
| TP-CASTT Analysis | Title, Paraphrase, Connotation, Attitude, Shifts, Title, Theme |
| Creative Writing Workshop | Guided writing using specific forms (free verse, sonnet, haiku) |
| Performance-Based Learning | Spoken word rehearsal and delivery |
| Comparative Analysis | Side-by-side examination of poems with shared themes |

**Assessments:**
- Formative: TP-CASTT analyses, poetry journal, peer feedback
- Summative: Poetry portfolio (3–5 original poems + artist's statement) + spoken word performance

---

### Module 5: Research & Informational Literacy (Weeks 25–30)

**Focus:** Research skills, source evaluation, informational writing, and digital literacy

**Description:** Students investigate a self-selected topic within a guided framework, learning to locate credible sources, evaluate bias, synthesize information, and present findings in a structured research report.

**Key Resources:**
- Library databases and curated online sources
- *They Say / I Say* templates (Graff & Birkenstein)
- Fact-checking and lateral reading guides

**Instructional Strategies:**
| Strategy | Description |
|---|---|
| Inquiry-Based Learning | Students develop their own research questions |
| CRAAP Test | Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose for source evaluation |
| Mini-Lessons + Conferences | Direct instruction paired with individual writing conferences |
| Peer Review Workshops | Structured feedback using rubric-aligned checklists |

**Assessments:**
- Formative: Annotated bibliography (5+ sources), outline, rough draft checkpoints
- Summative: Research report (1,500–2,000 words, MLA format) + works cited page

---

### Module 6: Drama, Media & Synthesis (Weeks 31–36)

**Focus:** Dramatic literature, media literacy, and cumulative skill application

**Description:** Students study a dramatic work and examine media texts for rhetorical techniques. The course concludes with a capstone synthesis project demonstrating cumulative learning.

**Suggested Texts:**
- *Romeo and Juliet* or *A Midsummer Night's Dream* — Shakespeare
- Film adaptation clips for comparative analysis
- Selected media/advertising texts

**Instructional Strategies:**
| Strategy | Description |
|---|---|
| Readers' Theater / Performance | Performing scenes to deepen comprehension |
| Film vs. Text Comparison | Analytical viewing guides for directorial vs. authorial choices |
| Media Literacy Deconstruction | Analyzing digital content for bias, audience, and persuasion |
| Capstone Project (Choice Board) | Multimedia presentation, creative adaptation, analytical essay, or podcast |

**Assessments:**
- Formative: Scene performance, media analysis short responses, project checkpoints
- Summative: Capstone synthesis project + reflection essay

### Rubric: Literary Analysis Essay (20 pts)

| Criteria | Exemplary (5) | Proficient (4) | Developing (3) | Beginning (1–2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Thesis & Claim** | Clear, insightful thesis addressing theme and devices | Clear thesis on theme + 2 devices | Vague thesis, partially addresses prompt | No clear thesis or off-topic |
| **Textual Evidence** | 3+ well-chosen quotes, seamlessly integrated | 2–3 relevant quotes, adequate explanation | 1–2 quotes, surface-level explanation | No quotes or irrelevant evidence |
| **Analysis Depth** | Insightful connections beyond the obvious | Solid analysis linking devices to theme | Some analysis, mostly summary | Little to no analysis |
| **Organization** | Logical flow, effective transitions | Organized with some transitions | Loosely organized, weak transitions | Disorganized, hard to follow |

### Rubric: Argumentative Essay (25 pts)

| Criteria | Exemplary (5) | Proficient (4) | Developing (3) | Beginning (1–2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Claim & Focus** | Precise, compelling claim maintained throughout | Clear claim, minor lapses | Unfocused or shifting claim | No discernible claim |
| **Evidence & Sources** | 3+ credible pieces, properly cited | 2 relevant pieces with citations | 1 piece or weak/uncited sources | No evidence |
| **Counterclaim & Rebuttal** | Strongest counterclaim dismantled with logic | Reasonable rebuttal addressed | Weak rebuttal | No counterclaim |
| **Reasoning & Logic** | Flawless logical connections | Sound reasoning, minor gaps | Some logical gaps | Unclear reasoning, fallacies |
| **Conventions & Style** | Error-free, formal academic tone | Few errors, mostly formal | Several errors, occasional clarity issues | Frequent errors impede understanding |

### Rubric: Speaking & Listening (20 pts)

| Criteria | Exemplary (5) | Proficient (4) | Developing (3) | Beginning (1–2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Seminar Participation** | 4+ substantive contributions citing text | 3 contributions referencing text | 1–2 with limited support | Silent or off-topic |
| **Presentation Content** | Clear thesis, compelling evidence, logical structure | Clear thesis, adequate evidence | Thin or disorganized evidence | No clear thesis |
| **Delivery & Presence** | Confident eye contact, clear projection, natural pace | Good eye contact, audible | Inconsistent, occasionally hard to hear | Reads from notes, inaudible |
| **Visual Aid** | Enhances understanding, creative, well-designed | Supports argument adequately | Loosely connected to content | Missing or distracting |

## Pacing Guide

### Yearly Time Distribution

| Module | Weeks | Content Delivery | Practice | Assessment |
|--------|:-----:|:-------:|:--------:|:----------:|
| 1 — Narrative | 1–6 | 28% | 48% | 24% |
| 2 — Argument & Persuasion | 7–12 | 30% | 48% | 22% |
| 3 — Novel Study | 13–18 | 26% | 52% | 22% |
| 4 — Poetry & Figurative Language | 19–24 | 27% | 52% | 21% |
| 5 — Research & Informational Literacy | 25–30 | 28% | 50% | 22% |
| 6 — Drama, Media & Capstone | 31–36 | 18% | 55% | 27% |
| **Year Average** | **36** | **~27%** | **~51%** | **~22%** |

### Sample Weekly Breakdown (Module 1, Week 2)

| Day | Content Delivery (20 min) | Practice (25 min) | Assessment (5 min) |
|-----|---|---|---|
| Mon | Theme & symbolism mini-lecture | Annotating short story in pairs | Exit ticket |
| Tue | Close reading model | Guided text-dependent questions | — |
| Wed | Literary device identification | Small-group analysis activity | Quick-write |
| Thu | Writing craft: showing vs. telling | Drafting analytical sentences | Peer check |
| Fri | Review & synthesis discussion | Independent close reading | Exit ticket |

### Key Pacing Principles

1. **Gradual Release Model** — Each module moves from heavier instruction early to practice and assessment by the end
2. **Practice-Heavy Design** — Over 50% of time is dedicated to active student practice
3. **Ongoing Formative Assessment** — Daily low-stakes checks (exit tickets, journals, peer feedback) to adjust instruction in real time
4. **Summative Anchors** — Each module concludes with a major assessment tied to a specific skill set
5. **Flex Days** — Build in 1–2 flex days per module for re-teaching, enrichment, or catch-up

## Classroom-Ready Worksheet: "The Power of Perspective"

### Part 1: Vocabulary in Context (10 pts) — *RL.9.4*

1. The protagonist's **ambivalence** about leaving home was clear — she packed her bags, then unpacked them three times.
   - a) excitement  b) mixed feelings  c) anger  d) certainty

2. His argument was **compelling**, and even his opponents found themselves nodding along.
   - a) boring  b) confusing  c) convincing  d) irrelevant

3. The author uses **juxtaposition** to highlight the difference between wealth and poverty.
   - a) placing contrasting elements side by side  b) exaggeration  c) repetition  d) a type of metaphor

4. After the scandal, the politician's **credibility** was completely destroyed.
   - a) wealth  b) trustworthiness  c) popularity  d) ambition

5. The tone shifts from optimistic to **somber** after the character receives the letter.
   - a) cheerful  b) serious and sad  c) angry  d) humorous

### Part 2: Reading Comprehension & Analysis (20 pts) — *RL.9.1, RL.9.2, RL.9.6*

Read the passage:

> "I used to think the world was divided into two kinds of people — those who watched things happen and those who made things happen. But standing at the edge of that river, with the current pulling at my shoes and the sky cracking open above me, I realized there was a third kind: those who never understood what was happening at all, and I had been one of them my entire life."

- [ ] Identify the point of view and explain its effect on the reader (2–3 sentences)
- [ ] Name one literary device, quote the text, and explain its effect
- [ ] State the emerging theme with at least one piece of textual evidence (3–4 sentences)

### Part 3: Grammar & Conventions (10 pts) — *L.9.1, L.9.2*

Correct the errors:

- "Neither the students nor the teacher **were** prepared for the fire drill."
- "The novel, which was published in 1960 have sold millions of copies worldwide."
- "Running through the park on a sunny afternoon, **the trees looked beautiful**." *(dangling modifier)*
- Combine: "Maya studied every night. She earned the highest grade in the class." *(subordinating conjunction)*

### Part 4: Argumentative Writing (20 pts) — *W.9.1*

**Prompt:** Should schools require a set list of classic novels, or should students choose their own reading material?

Write a well-organized argumentative paragraph (8–10 sentences) that includes:
- [ ] A clear claim
- [ ] At least two pieces of supporting evidence or reasoning
- [ ] A counterclaim and your response to it
- [ ] A concluding statement reinforcing your position

**Total: 60 points**

## Cross-Cutting Instructional Practices

- **Differentiation:** Tiered texts, flexible grouping, scaffolded graphic organizers, and choice in assessment format
- **Vocabulary Development:** Academic vocabulary instruction using Frayer models and contextual learning
- **Grammar in Context:** Conventions taught through student writing, not isolated drills
- **Formative Assessment Loops:** Regular exit tickets, self-assessments, and check-ins to adjust instruction
- **SEL Integration:** Journaling, collaborative norms, and texts that invite personal connection and empathy
