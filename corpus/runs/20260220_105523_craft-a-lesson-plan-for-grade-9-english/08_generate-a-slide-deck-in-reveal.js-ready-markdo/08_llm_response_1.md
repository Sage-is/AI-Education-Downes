## What Are Literary Devices?

> Literary devices are **techniques** that writers use to create special effects, convey meaning, and make their writing more vivid or persuasive.

They are the tools in every author's toolbox. 🔧

## Device #1: Simile

A **comparison** using *like* or *as*

--

### Simile — Example

> "The café was like a ship on the sea." 
> — Ernest Hemingway, *A Clean, Well-Lighted Place*

**Effect:** Creates a feeling of isolation and instability — the café drifts, unanchored, like the old man's sense of purpose.

--

### Simile — Your Turn

Read the following:

> "Her voice was like water in a desert — rare and life-giving."

- What two things are being compared? <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->
- What does the simile suggest about the character? <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->
- What mood does it create? <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->

---

## Device #2: Metaphor

A **direct comparison** — says something *is* something else

--

### Metaphor — Example

> "All the world's a stage, and all the men and women merely players."
> — William Shakespeare, *As You Like It*

**Effect:** Life is presented as a performance — suggesting our roles are temporary, scripted, and observed.

--

### Simile vs. Metaphor

| Simile | Metaphor |
|---|---|
| Uses *like* or *as* | Direct comparison |
| "Life is **like** a box of chocolates." | "Life **is** a box of chocolates." |
| Suggests similarity | Asserts identity |

Both create **vivid imagery** — but metaphors are often bolder.

---

## Device #3: Symbolism

When an object, character, or event **represents something beyond its literal meaning**

--

### Symbolism — Example

> In *Lord of the Flies*, the **conch shell** symbolizes order, democracy, and civilized authority.

When the conch is destroyed → civilization collapses. <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->

**Effect:** Golding tracks the boys' moral decline through a single, powerful object. <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->

--

### Symbolism — Think-Pair-Share

Can you think of a **symbol** from a book, film, or song you know?

- What is the object? <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->
- What does it represent? <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->
- How do you know? (What clues does the author give?) <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->

---

## Device #4: Imagery

Language that appeals to the **five senses** — sight, sound, touch, taste, smell

--

### Imagery — Example

> "The yellow smoke that rubs its muzzle on the window-panes, / Licked its tongue into the corners of the evening."
> — T.S. Eliot, *The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock*

| Sense | Detail |
|---|---|
| Sight | yellow smoke, window-panes |
| Touch | rubs its muzzle |
| Taste | licked its tongue |

**Effect:** The fog becomes a living, cat-like creature — the city feels eerie and alive. <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->

---

## Device #5: Foreshadowing

**Hints or clues** about events that will happen later in the story

--

### Foreshadowing — Example

> "I had the feeling that something was going to go terribly wrong, but I walked into the house anyway."

**Effect:** Builds **suspense and tension** — the reader feels dread before the event even occurs. <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->

--

### Foreshadowing — Spot It!

> In Chapter 1 of *Of Mice and Men*, Lennie carries a **dead mouse** in his pocket, petting it because he loves soft things.

What might this foreshadow about Lennie? <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->

*His inability to control his strength will lead to greater tragedy.* <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->

---

## Putting It All Together

### The P.E.E. Paragraph

| Step | Meaning | Example Starter |
|---|---|---|
| **P** — Point | State the device and its effect | "The author uses symbolism to..." |
| **E** — Evidence | Quote from the text | "This is shown when..." |
| **E** — Explain | Analyze *how* and *why* | "This suggests that... because..." |

--

### Model Paragraph

> **Point:** Golding uses the conch shell as a symbol of democratic order.
> **Evidence:** When Ralph holds the conch, "the boys obeyed," showing its authority.
> **Explain:** The conch's power mirrors civilized society — and when it shatters, so does any pretence of rules, reinforcing the novel's theme that savagery lies beneath the surface of humanity.

## Key Takeaways

1. Literary devices are **deliberate choices** — always ask *why* <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->
2. Move beyond **identifying** → focus on **analyzing effect** <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->
3. Use the **P.E.E. structure** to organize your analysis <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->
4. Great analysis connects devices to **theme, character, or mood** <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->
