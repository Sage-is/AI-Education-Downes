# 10-Minute YouTube Video: "How to Research Anything (Without Wasting Hours)"

**Target length:** ~10 minutes (≈1,450 words at a conversational 145 wpm)
**Tone:** friendly, practical, a little wry
**Visual style:** talking head + screen recordings + simple B-roll

---

## [00:00–00:45] HOOK

**[On camera, leaning in]**
"You ever fall into a research rabbit hole, four hours deep, and realize you have twelve browser tabs, one vague idea of what you were looking for, and a Wikipedia article about something completely unrelated? Yeah. Today we fix that. By the end of this video you'll have a repeatable system for researching anything — fast, and without the panic."

**[Lower third: "Research ≠ Googling.]**
"Because research is not just typing questions into a search bar. It's a skill. And like any skill, there's a method."

---

## [00:45–02:00] THE MINDSET SHIFT

**[B-roll: person staring at blank doc]**
"Most people fail at research before they start, because they begin with a topic instead of a question. 'I'm going to research climate change' is not research. It's a panic attack waiting to happen.

The fix: start with a question you actually want answered. Not 'climate change' — 'why do some cities flood more than others after the same storm?' Specific questions find specific answers. Vague topics find you a nap.

Write your question down. Seriously. One sentence. That sentence is your compass for the entire session. If a source doesn't help answer it, it's a distraction — close the tab."

---

## [02:00–03:30] STEP 1 — DIVERGE, THEN CONVERGE

**[Screen recording: search bar]**
"Step one is the 'diverge' phase. You want breadth before depth. Open a doc and dump everything: keywords, names, half-thoughts, related terms. Don't judge it.

Then go wide. Use more than Google. Try:
- **Google Scholar** or your library database for academic work
- **YouTube** for explainers and talks
- **Reddit, forums, Substack** for real-world experience
- **Books** — yes, actual books — for depth

Spend maybe 20–30% of your time here. The goal isn't to read everything. It's to find the *shape* of the conversation: who's arguing what, where the consensus is, where it's messy."

---

## [03:30–05:15] STEP 2 — EVALUATE SOURCES (THE FILTER)

**[Split screen: good source vs sketchy source]**
"This is where most people quietly fail. They read the first thing that agrees with them and stop. Don't be that person.

Quick credibility check — the CRAAP test:
- **Currency:** when was it published? Old data isn't always wrong, but know its age.
- **Relevance:** does it actually answer *your* question?
- **Authority:** who wrote it, and are they qualified?
- **Accuracy:** is it citing evidence? Can you verify the claims?
- **Purpose:** are they selling something, or arguing a side?

And one more rule I live by: **trace claims back to the source.** If an article says 'studies show,' go find the study. Blogs summarize; primary sources inform. The further you are from the original, the more distorted the signal."

---

## [05:15–06:45] STEP 3 — CAPTURE SMART (NOT HARD)

**[Screen recording: note app with source links]**
"Here's a mistake I made for years: highlighting everything. Your brain thinks highlighting = learning. It does not. It's decoration.

Instead, capture in your own words. For every note, write:
1. The claim
2. Why it matters for *your* question
3. The source link

That third one saves you at the end when you're writing and can't remember if that stat came from the paper or the podcast. Use a tool — Notion, Obsidian, even a plain doc. The tool doesn't matter. The *habit* of linking back does.

Pro tip: keep a 'parking lot' section for interesting tangents. Park them. Come back only if you have time. Your question is the boss."

---

## [06:45–08:15] STEP 4 — SYNTHESIZE (THE ACTUAL WORK)

**[On camera]**
"Collecting sources is not research. Connecting them is. This is the part nobody films for the aesthetic montage, because it's you, staring, thinking.

Take your notes and look for patterns:
- Where do sources agree? That's your foundation.
- Where do they clash? That's your *interesting* part — dig in.
- What's missing? Gaps are where original thinking lives.

Then write a one-paragraph answer to your original question — before you've 'done enough.' You'll never feel done. The draft exposes what you're actually missing, which tells you exactly what to research next. Research is a loop, not a line."

---

## [08:15–09:15] STEP 5 — WATCH YOUR BIAS

**[On camera, lighter tone]**
"Quick honesty check. Were you researching to *learn*, or to *win*? We all drift toward sources that confirm what we already believe. That's not research, that's a comfort blanket with citations.

Deliberately read one strong counter-argument. If it doesn't shake you a little, you're probably not looking hard enough. The best researchers aren't the ones who're right — they're the ones whose views can survive contact with the opposition."

---

## [09:15–10:00] WRAP + CTA

**[On camera]**
"So: question first, diverge then converge, filter with CRAAP, capture in your own words with links, synthesize by finding patterns and gaps, and check your bias. That's the whole system.

Pick something you've been meaning to learn and run it through this today — 30 minutes, no pressure. Then come back and tell me what you found.

If this saved you from a four-hour rabbit hole, smash that like, subscribe, and I'll see you in the next one."
